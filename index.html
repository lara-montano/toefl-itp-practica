Sesión 1 — Structure and Written Expression

Meta: resolver 40 reactivos en 25 minutos (unos 37 segundos por reactivo).

Structure: completar la oración

  1. Marca cada verbo conjugado y busca su sujeto.
  2. Cuenta cláusulas y conectores: conectores = cláusulas − 1 (and, but, because, although, which, that, when…).
  3. Descarta las opciones que dejan una cláusula sin sujeto o sin verbo, o que agregan un verbo sin conector.

Sesión 2 — Listening

Meta: reconocer las 8 trampas de los diálogos cortos y anticipar las preguntas de conversaciones y charlas. Todo se escucha una sola vez.

  1. Antes del audio, lee rápido las opciones de las cuatro preguntas: anticipan el tema y los datos que hay que buscar.

Sesión 3 — Reading

Meta: resolver un texto de 10 preguntas en 11 minutos, el ritmo del examen (50 preguntas en 55 minutos).

Método general. Lee el texto completo en 2 o 3 minutos para saber de qué trata cada párrafo. Después busca cada respuesta en el párrafo que indica la pregunta. Si una pregunta toma más de un minuto, marca una opción y sigue.

Tipos de pregunta

Idea principal
What does the passage mainly discuss?
Lee la primera oración de cada párrafo; descarta opciones demasiado específicas o demasiado amplias.
Detalle
According to paragraph 2, …
Busca en el texto una palabra clave de la pregunta y lee esa oración completa.
Vocabulario
The word X is closest in meaning to
Sustituye cada opción en la oración; la correcta conserva el sentido.
Referencia
The word "it" refers to
Busca el sustantivo anterior que concuerda en número y en sentido.
Inferencia
It can be inferred that …
No está escrito literal, pero se deduce de una oración concreta; descarta lo que exige suponer de más.
NOT / EXCEPT
Which is NOT mentioned?
Localiza las tres opciones que sí aparecen; la que sobra es la respuesta.
Propósito
Why does the author mention X?
Lee la oración anterior; X casi siempre ilustra esa idea.
Organización
Which best describes the organization?
Fíjate en cómo empieza cada párrafo.

Vocabulario y referencia son las más rápidas: si falta tiempo, respóndelas primero. En el examen real las preguntas citan números de línea; aquí citan párrafos.

Texto 1 (guiado): The Pony Express

[1] In the spring of 1860, a new mail service promised to carry letters across the western half of the United States faster than ever before. The Pony Express, as it was called, connected St. Joseph, Missouri, with Sacramento, California, a distance of roughly 3,000 kilometers. Before it began, mail traveling by stagecoach or by ship could take three weeks or more to reach California. The Pony Express reduced that time to about ten days.

[2] The system depended on speed and organization rather than on new technology. Along the route, the company built a chain of relay stations, spaced roughly 15 to 25 kilometers apart. At each station, a rider exchanged his tired horse for a fresh one in about two minutes and continued on his way. After covering between 120 and 160 kilometers, he handed the mail pouch to the next rider, who carried it through the night if necessary. To keep the horses fast, riders had to be small and light, and the company preferred young men.

[3] The work was dangerous. Riders crossed deserts, mountains, and rivers in every kind of weather, often alone. Yet despite the risks, the service lost very little mail. Its reliability made it famous, and stories about its riders became part of the legend of the American West.

[4] Fame, however, did not bring financial success. Sending a letter by Pony Express was expensive, so relatively few people used it, and the company lost money from the start. More importantly, a faster technology was already on its way. In October 1861, workers completed the first telegraph line across the continent, which could transmit a message from coast to coast in minutes. Only two days later, the Pony Express announced that it would end its service. It had operated for about eighteen months.

Texto 2 (11 minutos): Light in the Deep Sea

[1] Sunlight disappears quickly as it passes through seawater. Below about 200 meters, the ocean grows dim, and below 1,000 meters it is completely dark. Yet this darkness is not empty of light. Many of the animals that live there can produce their own light, a process known as bioluminescence. In some surveys of the deep sea, roughly three-quarters of the animals observed were capable of glowing.

[2] Bioluminescence is the result of a chemical reaction. Inside special cells, a compound called luciferin combines with oxygen with the help of an enzyme, releasing energy in the form of light. Because this reaction produces almost no heat, it is sometimes described as "cold light." Most marine bioluminescence is blue or blue-green. These colors travel farthest through water, and many deep-sea animals cannot see red light at all.

[3] Animals use their light in several ways. The anglerfish, for example, has a glowing lure that hangs in front of its mouth. Small fish that approach the light, mistaking it for food, are quickly swallowed. Other animals use light for defense. Certain shrimp release a cloud of glowing fluid when they are attacked, which may confuse a predator long enough for the shrimp to escape.

[4] One of the most surprising uses of light is camouflage. In the upper part of the deep sea, a small amount of sunlight still filters down from above. Predators looking up can see the dark shapes of animals against this faint light. To avoid being seen, some fish and squid produce light on their undersides that matches the light coming from above. This strategy, called counterillumination, makes them nearly invisible from below.

[5] Scientists are still discovering new examples of bioluminescence, and many questions remain. Because the deep sea is difficult and expensive to explore, much of the behavior of its glowing inhabitants has never been observed directly.

Texto 3 (11 minutos): The Birth of Impressionism

[1] In the nineteenth century, the most important event in the French art world was the Salon, an official exhibition held in Paris. A jury of established artists decided which works would be shown, and they generally preferred large, carefully finished paintings of historical, religious, or mythological subjects. Artists whose work was rejected by the Salon found it difficult to attract buyers or build a reputation.

[2] During the 1860s, a group of painters that included Claude Monet, Pierre-Auguste Renoir, and Camille Pissarro grew frustrated with these standards. Rather than painting scenes from the distant past, they wanted to show the world around them: city streets, gardens, rivers, and ordinary people at leisure. Above all, they were interested in light and in the way it changes the appearance of objects from one hour to the next.

[3] To capture these changing effects, the painters often worked outdoors instead of in a studio. This practice had become much easier after the invention of paint in metal tubes in the 1840s. Before then, oil paint was usually stored in pig bladders that burst easily and dried out quickly, which made painting outside impractical. Working quickly to record a passing moment, the painters used short, visible brushstrokes and bright, unmixed colors. To many viewers at the time, the results looked unfinished.

[4] In 1874, tired of rejection, the group organized its own exhibition. One of the works shown was Monet's Impression, Sunrise, a view of a harbor in morning fog. A critic who disliked the exhibition used the title of this painting to mock the entire group, calling its members "Impressionists." The painters soon adopted the name themselves.

[5] The 1874 exhibition was not a commercial success, and critics continued to attack the new style for years. Eventually, however, Impressionism gained wide acceptance, and it influenced many of the movements that followed.

Cómo se calcula el puntaje

Tus aciertos de cada sección se convierten con la tabla oficial a un puntaje de 31 a 68 (Reading, de 31 a 67). El total es (L + S + R) × 10 ÷ 3. Ejemplo: 50 + 52 + 51 = 153; 153 × 10 ÷ 3 = 510.
